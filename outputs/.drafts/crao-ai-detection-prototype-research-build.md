## Findings

### Q1. Agentic clinical triage: LLM orchestrating image models, checklists, escalation

- **EyeAgent (arXiv 2511.09394, 2025-11-12, preprint).** An LLM (DeepSeek-V3) is the "central reasoning engine" that "dynamically orchestrates 53 validated ophthalmic tools across 23 imaging modalities". Ablation: diagnostic accuracy rose "from a baseline of 69.71% (using only 5 general tools) to 80.79% when the full suite of 53 specialized tools was integrated". On 200 real-world cases it reached "93.7% tool selection accuracy" and expert ratings "≥88%" for accuracy, completeness, safety, reasoning and interpretability. As an assistant it "improved overall diagnostic accuracy by 18.51% and report quality scores by 19%, with the greatest benefit observed among junior ophthalmologists". [1] verified (abstract, full text fetched)
- **Agentic glaucoma framework (arXiv 2608.07651, 2026, preprint).** Three steps: "(1) LLM initial assessment, (2) function calling to invoke specialized deep learning tools including quality assessment (QAModel, FundaQ-8), glaucoma classification (SwinV2-Tiny), and optic disc/cup segmentation (SegFormer-B0), and (3) LLM reflection". Tested with Gemini 2.5 Flash and GPT-5.4 mini on ORIGA (n=100) and RIM-ONE-v3 (n=100). Results: the agentic workflow "improved glaucoma classification accuracy by 16–47 percentage points"; it matched the specialist's 88% accuracy on RIM-ONE-v3. LLM-alone failure modes: "GPT-5.4 mini demonstrated systematic positive bias (sensitivity 95–100%, specificity 0–5%), while Gemini 2.5 Flash showed stochastic variability between runs". Run-to-run kappa went "from near-random (κ as low as −0.01) to near-perfect agreement (κ up to 0.96)". [2] verified (abstract). Small test sets (n=100 each).
- **LLMs alone are not ready for autonomous decisions (Hager et al., Nat Med 2024, PMID 38965432).** On 2,400 MIMIC cases (four abdominal pathologies), LLMs "do not accurately diagnose patients across all pathologies (performing significantly worse than physicians), follow neither diagnostic nor treatment guidelines, and cannot interpret laboratory results". They "often fail to follow instructions and are sensitive to both the quantity and order of information". [3] verified (abstract)
- **LLMs are persuadable (arXiv 2608.29453, 2026, preprint).** "the same persuasive input changes about 10% more cases when it comes from a senior clinician than from a medical student", and "a plausible clinician view can persuade AI away from a correct decision even when it is fabricated to support an incorrect answer." [4] verified (abstract)
- **Humans over-rely on AI (automation bias).** In a prospective experiment, 27 radiologists read 50 mammograms with a purported AI that gave a wrong BI-RADS category for 12 of the 40 test cases. Accuracy fell sharply when the AI was wrong: inexperienced 79.7% vs 19.8%, moderately experienced 81.3% vs 24.8%, very experienced 82.3% vs 45.5% (all P ≤ .003) (Dratsch et al., Radiology 2023, PMID 37129490). [5] verified (abstract). A pathology study (n=28 experts) measured "a 7% automation bias rate", meaning correct independent estimates overturned by wrong AI advice. Time pressure "did not increase the frequency" of automation bias but "appeared to intensify its severity" (arXiv 2603.11821, 2026, preprint). [6] verified (abstract)

### Q2. Image-quality gating and lessons from Thai DR AI deployments

- **Beede et al., CHI 2020 (doi 10.1145/3313831.3376718).** Fieldwork at "11 clinics within the provinces of Pathum Thani and Chiang Mai during November 2018, April 2019, and August 2019", with the system deployed at three Pathum Thani clinics. Quote: "Out of 1838 images that were put through the system (in the first six months of usage), 393 (21%) didn't meet the system's high standards for grading." Causes: "fundus photos being taken in a non-darkened environment ... or from a camera that needed repair", and clinics "were not using dilation drops". Connectivity: on slow links "some images ... take 60-90 seconds to upload"; "In one clinic, the internet went out for a period of two hours during eye screening, reducing the number of patients screened from 200 to only 100." [7] verified (ACM full text retrieved through the Exa search index; dl.acm.org returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch). The Google blog companion post adds that an AI "might conservatively call some of these images 'ungradable' because the issues might obscure critical anatomical features" [8] verified (snippet).
- **Ruamviboonsuk et al., Lancet Digit Health 2022 (PMID 35272972; TCRT20190902002).** Prospective interventional cohort at nine primary-care sites in Thailand's national DR screening programme, Dec 2018 to Mar 2020. 7,940 patients screened; 7,651 (96.3%) analysed; 2,412 (31.5%) were referred "for diabetic retinopathy, diabetic macular oedema, ungradable images, or low visual acuity". For vision-threatening DR the DLS had accuracy 94.7% (95% CI 93.0–96.2), sensitivity 91.4% (87.1–95.0) and specificity 95.4% (94.1–96.7). Retina-specialist over-readers had accuracy 93.5, sensitivity 84.8% (p=0.024) and specificity 95.5%. PPV was 79.2 vs 75.6 and NPV 95.5 vs 92.4. The safety mechanism: "regional retina specialists over-read each image". Conclusion: "Socioenvironmental factors and workflows must be taken into consideration". [9] verified (abstract). The abstract does not give the ungradable fraction separately (gap).
- **EyeQ (Fu et al., MICCAI 2019, arXiv 1907.05345; repo github.com/HzFu/EyeQ).** "28,792 retinal images from the EyePACS dataset" re-annotated into three grades: "'Good', 'Usable' and 'Reject'". The split is train 12,543 (Good 8,347 / Usable 1,876 / Reject 2,320) and test 16,249 (Good 8,470 / Usable 4,559 / Reject 3,220). Best model was DenseNet121-MCS (multi-colour-space fusion): test accuracy 0.9175, F-measure 0.8551. ResNet18-RGB reached 0.8914. DR-detection accuracy dropped on lower-quality images, e.g. ResNet18 0.9014 (Good) to 0.8848 (Reject). [10] verified (full text). Licence: the README says the code is released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 "for NonCommercial use only". The images themselves must be downloaded from the EyePACS Kaggle competition [11] verified (README).
- **Quality gating inside an agent.** The glaucoma agent in [2] calls quality-assessment tools (QAModel, FundaQ-8) before classification. [2] verified (abstract)
- **Base CRAO paper exclusions.** The base paper excluded poor-quality and multi-pathology images, and used standard cameras only (from the T4 brief and `CRAO-AI-detection.md`). Inference: a deployed version will meet images the model never saw unless a quality gate rejects them.

### Q3. Uncertainty, selective prediction, OOD detection, calibration, abstain-and-escalate

- **Uncertainty-informed referral on fundus DR (Leibig et al., Sci Rep 2017, PMID 29259224).** Used dropout-based Bayesian uncertainty (MC dropout). "uncertainty informed decision referral can improve diagnostic performance". The models "surpass 85% sensitivity and 80% specificity as recommended by the NHS when referring 0-20% of the most uncertain decisions for further inspection". Caveat: "sensitivity to unfamiliar data samples is task dependent". [17] verified (abstract)
- **Modern networks are miscalibrated; temperature scaling fixes much of it (Guo et al., ICML 2017, arXiv 1706.04599).** "modern neural networks, unlike those from a decade ago, are poorly calibrated". Their recipe: "on most datasets, temperature scaling -- a single-parameter variant of Platt Scaling -- is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions." [18] verified (abstract)
- **Conformal prediction gives distribution-free coverage (Angelopoulos & Bates, arXiv 2107.07511).** It works "with any pre-trained model, such as a neural network, to produce sets that are guaranteed to contain the ground truth with a user-specified probability, such as 90%". The guarantees are "explicit, non-asymptotic ... even without distributional assumptions". [19] verified (abstract). Inference: the guarantee assumes calibration and test data are exchangeable, so it does not protect against a new camera or a new hospital.
- **OOD detectors miss near-distribution shifts (Cao et al., arXiv 2007.04250, "A Benchmark of Medical Out of Distribution Detection").** Benchmarked on chest X-ray, fundus and histology. Methods "fail to recognize images close to the training distribution"; "a simple binary classifier on the feature representation has the best accuracy and AUPRC on average". [20] verified (abstract)
- **Learned deferral to clinicians (CoDoC; Dvijotham et al., Nat Med 2023, PMID 37460754).** CoDoC learns when to trust the AI and when to defer to the clinical workflow. In UK breast screening it "reduced false positives by 25% at the same false-negative rate, while achieving a 66% reduction in clinician workload". In TB triage it gave a "5-15% reduction in false positives at the same false-negative rate for three of five commercially available predictive AI systems". [21] verified (abstract)
- **Abstention for fundus DR at fixed coverage (Chopra et al., arXiv 2605.19133, 2026, preprint).** Reports selective results "at a fixed coverage of 70%", described as "a clinically realistic trade-off". Datasets: EyePACS 35,000 (SSL pretraining), APTOS-19 3,662, Messidor 1,200, a 7-class fundus set of 757. Finding: "once accuracy saturates, selective performance can still change markedly across checkpoints, and longer pretraining does not consistently improve reliability". [22] verified (full text excerpt)
- **Selective autonomy for LLM agents (Zhang et al., "On-premise medical AI agents for reliable clinical decision-making", Nat Med 2026, PMID 42744896).** On MIMIC-IV benchmarks the on-premise agent reached "90.04% accuracy on a seven-disease task and 83.8% accuracy on a four-disease task". "At a consistency threshold of 0.90, 49.4% of cases were retained at 98.9% diagnostic accuracy". The remainder were deferred for review. [23] verified (abstract)

### Q4. Explainability (Grad-CAM and saliency): limits and what clinicians find useful

- **Base paper used Grad-CAM.** The base paper "applied" the "gradient-weighted class activation map" method and averaged heatmaps per class into "disease-specific class-activation maps" (`CRAO-AI-detection.md`). verified (local full text)
- **Saliency maps fail trustworthiness tests (Arun et al., Radiol Artif Intell 2021, PMID 34870212).** "All eight saliency map techniques failed at least one of the criteria and were inferior in performance compared with localization networks." On pneumothorax, saliency AUPRC ranged from 0.024 to 0.224 vs U-Net 0.404. On pneumonia it ranged from 0.160 to 0.519 vs RetinaNet 0.596. "Five and two saliency methods (of eight) failed the model randomization test". The authors recommend "detection or segmentation models be used if localization is the desired output". [12] verified (abstract)
- **Explainability as "false hope" (Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner, Beam, Lancet Digit Health 2021, PMID 34711379).** They argue current methods "are unlikely to achieve these goals for patient-level decision support". They advocate "rigorous internal and external validation" instead, and "caution against having explainability be a requirement for clinically deployed models". [13] verified (abstract)
- **What fundus readers actually gained (Sayres et al., Ophthalmology 2019, PMID 30553900).** Ten ophthalmologists read 1,796 images in three conditions: unassisted, grades only, grades plus heatmap (integrated gradients). Sensitivity for moderate-or-worse DR was 79.4% unassisted, 87.5% with grades only and 88.7% with grades plus heatmap, with no matching specificity drop (96.6% / 96.1% / 95.5%). "Grades plus heatmaps improved accuracy for patients with DR (P < 0.001), but reduced accuracy for patients without DR (P = 0.006)", and "For most cases, grades plus heatmap was only as effective as grades only." Assistance increased grading time and confidence. [14] verified (abstract)
- **Grad-CAM is the best of a weak field, but still below humans (Saporta et al., Nat Mach Intell 2022, doi 10.1038/s42256-022-00536-x).** Seven saliency methods were tested on chest X-ray. "while Grad-CAM generally localized pathologies better than the other evaluated saliency methods, all seven performed significantly worse compared with the human benchmark". The gap "was largest for pathologies that were smaller in size and had shapes that were more complex". "model confidence was positively correlated with Grad-CAM localization performance". [15] verified (abstract on nature.com)
- **Visual plausibility is not evidence (Adebayo et al., NeurIPS 2018, arXiv 1810.03292).** "reliance, solely, on visual assessment can be misleading"; "some existing saliency methods are independent both of the model and of the data generating process." [16] verified (abstract)

### Q5. Fast prototype stack: Gradio vs Streamlit, Hugging Face Spaces, timm, fine-tuning compute

- **Hugging Face Spaces, current rules (fetched 2026-10-17).** "Static Spaces are free for everyone. Gradio and Docker Spaces run on compute and require a paid plan to create: PRO for personal accounts, Team or Enterprise for organizations. Free personal accounts in good standing can still host up to 2 Gradio Spaces running on ZeroGPU." [27] verified. Hardware table: CPU Basic is 2 vCPU, 16 GB, "Free!", but "creating a new Space that runs on compute (Gradio or Docker) requires a paid plan". Nvidia T4 small is 4 vCPU, 15 GB RAM, 16 GB GPU at $0.40/h; T4 medium is $0.60/h; 1× L4 is $0.80/h [26] verified. PRO costs "$9 /month" [28] verified. ZeroGPU "dynamically allocates and releases NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell GPUs" (docs snippet) [26] verified (snippet only). The SDK options listed are "Gradio, Docker and static HTML"; Streamlit is not listed as a native SDK on the overview page [27] verified.
- **Gradio sharing.** `launch(share=True)` gives a public `*.gradio.live` link. The Gradio share servers "are only a proxy for your local server, and do not store any data". "Share links expire after 1 week", and "share links are publicly accessible". Password protection: `demo.launch(auth=("admin", "pass1234"))` [29] verified (docs).
- **Streamlit Community Cloud.** Limits "as of February 2024": "CPU: 0.078 cores minimum, 2 cores maximum Memory: 690MB minimum, 2.7GBs maximum Storage: No minimum, 50GB maximum". "All apps without traffic for 12 hours go to sleep." [30] verified. `st.file_uploader` limits uploads to "200 MB each" by default [31] verified.
- **timm fine-tuning entry point.** `timm.create_model('mobilenetv3_large_100', pretrained=True, num_classes=NUM_FINETUNE_CLASSES)`. "To fine-tune on your own dataset, you have to write a PyTorch training loop or adapt timm's training script". [32] verified (docs)
- **RETFound: retina foundation model, and the one fundus fine-tuning compute figure found (Zhou et al., Nature 2023, PMID 37704728, PMC10550819).** Pretrained on "1.6 million unlabelled retinal images". Pretraining used "eight NVIDIA Tesla A100 (40 GB)" GPUs for "2 weeks". Fine-tuning: "We required only one NVIDIA Tesla T4 (16 GB) GPU, requiring about 1.2 h with a dataset of 1,000 images." Downstream recipe: ViT-large encoder plus MLP head, label smoothing, "batch size is 16", "total training epoch is 50", warm-up over 10 epochs to lr 5 × 10^-4, then cosine decay to 1 × 10^-6, with the best validation AUROC checkpoint kept. [24] verified (PMC full text). Label efficiency: comparable to other groups "with 45% of data in diabetic retinopathy MESSIDOR-2" [24] verified.
- **RETFound access and licence.** The repo `rmaphoh/RETFound` LICENSE is "Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 International". The weights are on Hugging Face (e.g. `YukunZhou/RETFound_mae_natureCFP`), gated (`gated: auto`) behind a form asking name, affiliation and job title. The README example fine-tune command uses `--batch_size 24 --epochs 50 --input_size 224` on one GPU, and links a Colab notebook ("free GPU") [25] verified (GitHub README, LICENSE, HF API).
- **EyeQ as the quality-gate training set.** Quality labels are `Label_EyeQ_train.csv` / `Label_EyeQ_test.csv`; the images come from EyePACS on Kaggle. The README warns: "The original weights of MCF-Net are no longer usable due to several version updates. We recommend re-training the model". [11] verified. A Hugging Face model search for "fundus quality", "retinal image quality" and "eyeq" returned no fundus-quality classifier (only an unrelated LLM). So no ready-made quality-gate checkpoint was found on the Hub (verified absence for those three queries only).
- **Base paper code and data.** Data are "available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request" (`CRAO-AI-detection.md`). The base paper's model is a custom CNN ("strided and classic convolutional layers ... fully connected layers, and a SoftMax layer") with dropout, and its reference 41 is a GitHub app `milealeonard/Classif-Eye`. That repo exists (last push 2020-04-13, no licence file reported by the GitHub API) [42] verified.

### Q6. Reporting and regulation

- **TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., BMJ 2024;385:e078378, PMID 38626948).** Presents "the expanded 27 item checklist" plus "the TRIPOD+AI for Abstracts checklist", for studies that develop or evaluate prediction models using regression or machine learning. [33] verified (abstract)
- **CONSORT-AI (Liu et al., Nat Med 2020, PMID 32908283).** "includes 14 new items" on top of CONSORT 2010. It asks for descriptions of "instructions and skills required for use, the setting in which the AI intervention is integrated, the handling of inputs and outputs of the AI intervention, the human-AI interaction and provision of an analysis of error cases". [34] verified (abstract)
- **SPIRIT-AI (Cruz Rivera et al., Nat Med 2020, PMID 32908284).** "includes 15 new items" for trial protocols, on top of SPIRIT 2013 [35] verified (abstract)
- **DECIDE-AI (Vasey et al., Nat Med 2022, PMID 35585198).** For early-stage live clinical evaluation of AI decision support: "17 AI-specific reporting items (made of 28 subitems) and ten generic reporting items". Its purpose includes evaluating "the human factors surrounding its use" [36] verified (abstract)
- **WHO, Ethics and governance of AI for health (2021).** Six principles: "Protecting human autonomy" ("humans should remain in control of health-care systems and medical decisions"); "Promoting human well-being and safety and the public interest"; "Ensuring transparency, explainability and intelligibility"; "Fostering responsibility and accountability" (including "mechanisms ... for questioning and for redress"); "Ensuring inclusiveness and equity"; "Promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable" [37] verified (WHO news release 28-06-2021). WHO also has LMM-specific guidance, "Guidance on large multi-modal models"; its publication page shows "25 March 2025" [38] verified (page).
- **Thai FDA SaMD/AI guidance (Medical Device Control Division, "คู่มือแนวทางการกำกับดูแลซอฟต์แวร์และปัญญาประดิษฐ์ที่เป็นเครื่องมือแพทย์", published "ตุลาคม 2567" = October 2024; Thai PDF).** Regulation under the Medical Device Act B.E. 2551 (as amended, incl. No. 2 B.E. 2562) moved from policy-based to risk-based control. SaMD/AI moved from "general medical device" to "listing" (Class 1) or "notification" (higher classes) routes. Classification examples under Rule 10(1): Class 2 is for non-critical conditions and includes "ซอฟต์แวร์ปัญญาประดิษฐ์ช่วยในการตรวจคัดกรองภาวะเบาหวานขึ้นตา" (AI software assisting diabetic-retinopathy screening). Class 3 is for diagnosing or screening "สภาวะของโรคที่วิกฤต" (critical conditions) and includes "ซอฟต์แวร์ช่วยวินิจฉัยโรคผู้ป่วยหลอดเลือดสมองขาด Stroke" (ischaemic-stroke diagnosis software). The guide requires describing input specs and any pre-processing (e.g. "image resizing") with justification. It tells developers to follow PDPA B.E. 2562 [39] verified (PDF text, extracted with pdftotext; two-column Thai layout, so table cells were partly interleaved; medium confidence on the exact cell mapping). A consultancy summary (Qualtechs) agrees: Class 2 for "non-serious conditions", Class 3 for "immediate danger situations (e.g., stroke diagnosis software ...)". It adds that devices with connectivity "must provide cybersecurity evidence", that "AI/ML devices must disclose datasets used for training and testing", and that they "should not replace clinical judgment" [40] secondary, verified (page).
- **Thai PDPA Section 26 (health data = sensitive data).** Collecting "health data ... biometric data" "is prohibited, without the explicit consent from the data subject", with exceptions. These include preventing danger to life when the subject cannot consent; compliance with law for "preventive medicine ... medical diagnosis, the provision of health or social care, medical treatment"; and "scientific, historical, or statistic research purposes ... only to the extent necessary ... and the suitable measures have been provided ... as prescribed by the Committee" [41] verified (English text on pdpathailand.com, an unofficial translation, not the Royal Gazette; confidence medium).

## Implications for the enhanced version and the easy prototype (all INFERENCE unless cited)

1. **Use a deterministic orchestrator around specialist models. The LLM should only explain, never decide.** Evidence: LLM-alone fundus reading showed "systematic positive bias" and run-to-run randomness, both fixed by tool calls [2]; LLMs fail guidelines and instructions [3] and can be persuaded by fabricated clinician views [4]. Prototype pipeline:
   `upload → strip metadata (PDPA) → quality gate (Good/Usable/Reject) → OOD check → 4-class CNN (CRAO/CRVO/NAION/normal) → temperature-scaled probability → abstain/escalate rule → (optional) LLM writes a checklist summary from structured outputs only`.
   The escalation rule is code, not prompt: e.g. if P(CRAO) is above threshold *and* onset is under 4.5 h, show a "stroke-pathway alert, call neuro/ophtho now" banner. The LLM output is read-only text built from the structured fields, and the classifier result is shown separately so the LLM cannot alter it.
2. **Put the quality gate first and give a "retake photo" prompt, not a silent failure.** In Thai clinics 21% of images were rejected, driven by room lighting, camera faults and no dilation [7]. Train a small timm ResNet18 or DenseNet121 on EyeQ (MCF-Net reached 0.9175 accuracy [10]; the README says to retrain, since the old weights are unusable [11]). Show the reason ("too dark / blurred, please darken the room and retake"). This also covers the base paper's excluded poor-quality images.
3. **Abstain and escalate instead of forcing four classes.** Apply temperature scaling on a held-out split [18] and use either an uncertainty threshold [17] or a split-conformal prediction set [19]. If the set contains more than one class (e.g. {CRAO, CRVO}) or confidence is low, output "uncertain, urgent specialist review" rather than a label. Add a cheap OOD check, such as a binary classifier on embeddings ([20] found it best on average), to catch smartphone, widefield or OCT inputs the model never saw. Report coverage vs sensitivity (as in [22]) rather than one AUC.
4. **Explainability: label Grad-CAM as "where the model looked", not as a lesion map.** Saliency fails randomization and localization tests [12][15][16]. Heatmaps reduced accuracy on normal images in a fundus reader study [14]. Prefer showing calibrated probabilities plus quality and uncertainty flags; offer the heatmap as an optional toggle. For a hackathon demo, "honest uncertainty + over-read workflow" is more defensible than heatmaps [13].
5. **Design against automation bias.** Readers' accuracy collapsed when the AI was wrong [5][6]. Ideas: a "clinician first impression" field before revealing the AI output (an inference adapted from the independent-then-assisted protocol in [6]), always-visible specialist over-read (as in the Thai DR programme [9]), and an error-case log (CONSORT-AI asks for "analysis of error cases" [34]).
6. **Pick the model backbone.** For the 24–48 h *easy* prototype: timm `create_model(..., pretrained=True, num_classes=4)` [32] on ImageNet weights is the least friction. The *enhanced* version can use RETFound, which is fundus-pretrained and label-efficient [24]. One T4 needed about 1.2 h per 1,000 images for ViT-L fine-tuning [24], so a 1–2 k image set fits in a hackathon. But the weights are gated and the code is CC BY-NC 4.0 [25], which is fine for a non-commercial demo and blocks commercial use without permission.
7. **Deployment for the demo.** Run Gradio locally with `share=True` and `auth=` for judges [29]. Use only de-identified, public or synthetic images, because share links are public [29]. A new Gradio Space on CPU Basic needs PRO ($9/mo), while a free account can host up to 2 ZeroGPU Gradio Spaces [27][28]. Streamlit Community Cloud caps memory at about 2.7 GB [30], which may be too small for ViT-L weights (UNVERIFIED; measure checkpoint size). Make it offline-capable: clinics lost half their throughput when the internet failed [7].
8. **Regulatory framing for the pitch.** Thai FDA lists AI DR screening as Class 2 and ischaemic-stroke diagnosis software as Class 3 [39][40]. A tool flagging hyperacute CRAO (an eye-stroke emergency) for thrombolysis-window triage is plausibly Class 3, the "critical condition" class. This is inference only: it needs a Thai FDA classification query, and the exact rule mapping is unconfirmed. Report development per TRIPOD+AI [33] and plan a pilot per DECIDE-AI [36]. Map the design to the WHO principles: humans stay in control, transparency, and redress [37].
9. **Data governance.** Fundus photos are health (and possibly biometric) data under PDPA s.26, so use explicit consent or a documented exception such as research with safeguards [41]. Strip EXIF and patient IDs before upload. Do not put real patient images on a public share link [29].

## Conflicts and gaps

- **Ungradable rate not comparable across Thai studies.** Beede reports 21% (393/1,838) rejected images at three Pathum Thani clinics over the first six months [7]. Ruamviboonsuk's abstract only gives a combined 31.5% referral rate covering DR, DMO, ungradable images and low visual acuity across nine sites [9]. These are different denominators, sites and periods. Do not quote 31.5% as an ungradable rate. The separate ungradable rate in [9] is UNVERIFIED (full text not read; abstract only).
- **Beede full text was read via a search-index copy.** dl.acm.org returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch, so the quotes come from the Exa-indexed ACM HTML and match the companion Google blog [8]. Treat as verified text from a mirror.
- **WHO LMM guidance date.** The publication page shows "25 March 2025". An earlier (January 2024) release date is commonly cited but was not verified here, so the date is UNVERIFIED.
- **Thai FDA class for CRAO tools.** There is no CRAO-specific example in the guide. The Class 3 suggestion is inference by analogy to the stroke example. The PDF table extraction interleaved columns, so confidence is medium.
- **PDPA text.** Taken from an unofficial English translation site, not the Royal Gazette Thai original. Subordinate notifications from the PDPC on research safeguards were not checked.
- **Fine-tuning compute for ResNet/ViT-B on 1–2 k images.** Only RETFound's ViT-L figure (T4, ~1.2 h per 1,000 images) was found [24]. Smaller timm models should be faster, but that is UNVERIFIED and not measured here.
- **Recent agent papers are mostly preprints.** [1], [2], [4], [6] and [22] are arXiv preprints (2025–2026) with small or single-centre evaluations (e.g. n=100 per dataset in [2]). The peer-reviewed anchors are [3], [5], [21] and [23].
- **EyePACS/Kaggle data licence terms** for EyeQ images were not checked (UNVERIFIED). Only the EyeQ code licence (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) was read [11].
- **No public CRAO dataset was checked in this brief.** Dataset availability belongs to other researchers' briefs, and the base paper's data are "upon reasonable request".
